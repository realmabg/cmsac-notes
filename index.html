1. Was It the Player or the Team? — Bayesian skill modeling

Alexandre Andorra

Summary

Building a Bayesian model to estimate scoring skill while accounting for measured team context

Data
  • Player-match data from the Premier League, La Liga, Bundesliga, and Serie A, covering 2000–01 through 2023–24.
How the model works
  • Bayesian updating: combine prior beliefs with observed results to estimate a distribution of plausible scoring probabilities.
  • Partial pooling: learn player abilities together. Small samples are pulled more toward the group average instead of being treated as equally reliable.
  • Team context: account for home advantage, standings-points difference, and goal-difference difference between teams.
  • Change over time: Gaussian processes model smooth within-season and career-stage patterns. These curves are shared across players; they do not estimate individual hot streaks.
SAR and PAR: the practical outputs
  • Skill Above Replacement (SAR): player’s estimated scoring probability versus the average player after equalizing the measured team conditions.

    Hypothetical example: After equalizing team conditions, a player is estimated to score in 40% of matches and the average player in 30%. The player’s SAR is +0.10, or 10 percentage points above average.

  • Performance Above Replacement (PAR): player’s estimated scoring probability versus the average player, averaged over the player’s actual matches and team conditions.

    Hypothetical example: In that player’s actual team conditions, their estimated scoring probability averages 45%, compared with a dataset average of 32% under actual conditions. Their PAR is +0.13, or 13 percentage points above average.

Takeaways and limitations
  • Accounting for team context changed the estimated scoring probabilities more than it changed the overall player rankings. Some top players had overlapping ranges of plausible skill, so the model could not clearly establish who was better.
  • The model adjusts only for the factors included. Team style and teammate quality are not fully separated from player ability.
  • The shared career-stage curve can be affected by survivorship: players who decline may leave the leagues being studied.
Potential applications/ideas
  • Player skill: use past data and different priors to estimate a player’s three-point percentage (3PT%), assist percentage (AST%), or other efficiency measures with a Bayesian model suited to the statistic.
  • Basketball adaptation: use a similar approach on a metric to try to separate the player’s individual skill from other factors (opponent, role, etc.).
2. From Dots to Skeletons: How 3D Tracking Is Redefining What’s Possible in Basketball

Steven Mintz

Summary

Using Hawk-Eye technology, NBA tracking captures 29 points on each player’s body, 60 times per second. Compared with 2D floor positions, full-body tracking can show whether a defender’s hand is up, which way they are facing, how they affect a shooter’s view, etc.

Examples discussed

Some of these have already been implemented by the NBA.

  • Shot difficulty / expected FG%: use contest height, hip stance, speed, hand-to-ball distance, and vision interference, etc.
  • Offensive pressure: measure how closely a player is guarded.
  • Defensive pressure: a player’s or the ball’s distance from a player.
  • Gravity: measure the defensive attention a player draws relative to what the situation would predict.
  • Dunk Score: combines jump, power, style, and defense using tracking features.
  • Event detection: uses include goaltending detection and determining whether a shot is a three-pointer.
Potential applications/ideas
  • College shot quality and gravity: develop similar measures for NCAA.
  • Play recognition: identify recurring sets, including baseline out of bounds (BLOB) and sideline out of bounds (SLOB) plays.
  • Matchup analysis: score offensive–defensive pairs to find the best assignments.
Limitations
  • 3D data has around 90 times the data volume of 2D tracking and requires around 3 GB per game.
  • Hawk-Eye is too expensive for a single college program. A more affordable approach would be to build a computer vision model using practice and game video. This would provide 2D data, but would still be good enough to develop some metrics and applications, such as gravity and play recognition.
Data and resources
  • MLSE Sport Performance Lab: free data on free throw biomechanics.
  • SkillCorner basketball open data: free tracking and event data for 10 games from a Spanish professional league.
  • Paid/licensed data sources: NBA and Sportradar.
  • NBA metric glossary and gravity explanation: NBA metrics used; no public raw tracking data.
3. Bayesian Hierarchical Change Point Model for Pitcher Biomechanics

Rose Graves

Summary

A change point is a point where a statistical property, such as mean or variance, changes. This project looks for shifts in pitching mechanics, motivated by the hypothesis that pitchers may continue pitching while fatigued.

This study looks at pitcher biomechanics to find change points.

The Bayesian model includes game, player, and league effects. It can estimate change points for individual games and examine how consistently they occur for each pitcher.

Study details
  • Simulation: 30 pitchers, 30 starts, and a minimum of 100 pitches per start, focusing on fastball pitches.
  • League-level result: a change point around the 28th–32nd fastball, approximately 70 pitches overall.
  • Individual responses: players may have individual changes to their mechanics, for example increasing their load to make up for lost velocity. Many of the confidence intervals included zero, so effects should be interpreted with their uncertainty.
  • Future model extensions: additional distributions, autoregressive terms, and multiple variables.

A detected shift by itself does not prove fatigue or injury risk.

Potential applications/ideas
  • Identify repeatable movement changes: use 3D tracking to examine load-related changes within a stint. The movement must be repetitive enough to compare; shots alone may not be enough for some players.
  • Explore rotation timing: study whether patterns could help evaluate how long a player should be left in. For example, five versus eight minute shifts, or putting a player in with four versus six minutes left in the game.
  • Return from injury: explore how a player’s mechanics change when returning from an injury, helping monitor potential overexertion.
  • Connect mechanics to performance: examine whether shifts coincide with changes in shooting, blocking, boxing out, etc.

A first step would be to choose a frequent, reliably measured movement, establish each player’s baseline, and test whether there are shifts across games.

4. Quantifying Linebacker Bite on Play Action Passes

Tate Helms, William Sorg

Summary

“Bite” describes how much a linebacker reacts to a play-action fake. This project examines linebacker tendencies using their position at the snap and at the fake handoff.

Data & methods
  • 2021–25 seasons: approximately 90,000 plays and 150,000 observations.
  • Mixed-effects model with random effects for linebacker, defense, quarterback, and running back.
  • Predictors included pre-snap movement and pass probability.
Findings
  • Man coverage had higher bite than zone coverage.
  • Pre-snap movement and pass probability were the strongest predictors of bite velocity.
  • Linebackers accounted for most of the model’s non-residual variability.
  • Similarities in tendencies continue with coaches.
Limitations
  • Did not distinguish play action from run-pass options.
  • No player-level blitz identifier.
  • Did not compare fake handoffs with real run plays.
Potential applications/ideas
  • Pump fakes: see if a defender falls for a pump fake.
  • Dribble handoffs: examine how players or roles in defensive schemes react to dribble handoff situations.
5. Screen Time: Pre-Snap Play Prediction in the NFL

Krish Bothra, Ethan Larimore, Jenna Lau

Summary

Breaks down screen-pass prediction into three questions: is the play a screen, which direction will it go, and will the target be a running back/tight end or a wide receiver?

Methods
  • Model 1 — screen pass or not: team historical tendencies, play context, and spatial positioning.
  • Model 2 — thrown to the left or right: formation asymmetry, eligible receivers on each side, and the running back’s location relative to the quarterback.
  • Model 3 — thrown to running back/tight end or wide receiver.
Potential applications/ideas

On a SLOB or BLOB, predict where the ball will be inbounded and what set will be run.

6. When Faceoffs Matter Most: Quantifying Faceoff Value and Identifying High-Leverage Performance in the NHL

Chloe Guagliano, Tess Hinkle, Jack Pallotta

Summary

Rather than counting every faceoff win equally, estimate the net expected-goal value associated with a faceoff outcome and consider the game situation’s leverage.

Methods & findings
  • Generalized additive models (GAMs).
  • Leverage combines goal value and likelihood.
  • Some players won more faceoffs in higher-leverage situations.
  • Strong-side versus weak-side faceoffs did not explain the difference between high and low leverage win percentages.
Limitations & future work
  • The analysis used a five-second window after the faceoff.
  • Not all players had a high enough sample size of high-leverage faceoffs.
  • Future work mentioned predicting whether the next event after a given event would be a shot.
Potential applications/ideas
  • Jump balls: examine how important jump balls are in certain scenarios (low sample size issue)
  • Challenges: examine how important a challenge outcome is given the game scenario.
7. Pitch Quality Modeling: Controlling for Bat Path and Applying Results to Matchup Decisions

Vivian Du, Carla Martínez Santiago

Summary

Examines pitch quality while accounting for bat path, with applications to matchup decisions. Improves expected run value (ERV) prediction and helps distinguish batter contribution from the pitch itself.

Findings
  • Stronger ERV prediction after adding bat-tracking features.
Potential applications/ideas
  • Shot quality: combine shooting mechanics, such as release height and body position, with contest information to try to find the shooter’s skill independent of defensive pressure.
  • Matchup analysis: examine which defenders are associated with lower shot quality for particular shooters after accounting for the shooter’s mechanics, rather than judging only by makes and misses.
8. Increasing Offensive Threat with Off-ball Runs

Chaely Raynor, Reagan Gonzales

Summary

Examines which players create attacking value through off-ball runs, using roughly 500 MLS matches and 170,000 modeling observations. Ten run types were consolidated into five groups.

Methods
  • Features included distance to goal, run characteristics, proximity to the ball carrier and defensive line, game state, and other runners moving at the same time.
  • The target was expected threat (xThreat). Beta regression and beta generalized linear models were used with five-fold cross-validation.
  • Models progressively added run, spatial, and game-context features to a distance-based baseline. The full feature set performed best.
  • A linear model performed worse. A GAM was chosen to capture nonlinear relationships.
Findings
  • Distance to goal, speed up to a threshold, and a “sweet spot” of separation from defenders were associated with threat.
  • More simultaneous runs were associated with increased threat.
  • Threat over expectation highlighted players and teams that excelled off the ball.
  • Columbus Crew won in 2023 despite relatively low xThreat, raising the possibility that runs create other value, such as more space for the dribbler.
Limitations & future work
  • Results depend on the underlying xThreat model, which may miss some benefits of a run.
  • Only one season was studied.
  • Future work: richer tracking data, mixed-effects models for team and player differences, and XGBoost.
Potential applications/ideas
  • Off-ball cuts: use a similar model to estimate how a cut changes the offense’s scoring opportunities, accounting for the cutter’s location, speed, defender separation, and other players’ movement.
  • Spacing: examine how off-ball positioning and movement draw defenders away to create driving lanes or open shots for teammates, even when the player never receives the ball.
9. Quantifying Passing Decision Quality Under Pressure

Julia Biesiada, Swayam Chaulagain

Summary

Using SkillCorner data from 502 matches, this project compares the threat of the passing option selected with the most dangerous available option.

Data & methods
  • Event data: player possessions, passing options, and off-ball runs.
  • Tracking data: positions of players and ball.
  • xThreat: defined here as the probability of a goal within 10 seconds if a player receives the pass.
  • Passing threat reduction (PTR): compares the best available option with the actual choice.
  • Spatial measures included a four-meter passing-lane definition, defender distance to the passer, defender distance to the best option, and defender proximity to the passing lane.
  • Models: logistic regression, GAM, generalized additive mixed model, and XGBoost.
Findings & future work
  • Defender distance to the best option, pressing context, and defender distance to the passing lane were associated with the probability of choosing the best pass.
  • Future work: model continuous PTR, examine team-level patterns, and include turnover penalties.
Potential applications/ideas

Basketball: compare passing choices with the options available at different moments, including when the pass is made, to identify missed potential assists.

10. The Effects of Transferring on Player Performance: An Analysis on NCAA Division I Men’s Basketball

An Le, Kendall Lucas

Summary

Examines whether transferring improves individual performance, using seasons 2021–22 through 2025–26 and data accessed with hoopR. The main outcome was points per game (PPG).

Methods
  • Match transferred players with non-transferred players who had similar statistics.
  • Mixed-effects regression included transfer status, PPG, position, experience, conference, team rating, and conference rating.
Findings
  • Transfers often outperformed non-transfers, but the adjusted model found no large or statistically significant overall transfer effect.
  • Players above their team’s median PPG before transferring had their PPG decline by 1.54%; other players were reported to increase their PPG by 1.89%.
Limitations
  • PPG captures only one aspect of performance.
  • Complex transfer histories and non-athletic reasons for transferring were not fully accounted for.
  • Heteroskedasticity and nonlinear relationships were noted.
Potential applications/ideas
  • Broader performance metrics: look beyond minutes per game (MPG) and points per game to examine efficiency or all-in-one performance statistics.
  • Player–team fit: examine whether a transfer’s performance depends on how their playing style fits the new team’s style or the role of a departing player they are replacing.
11. Challenge or Pass? A Dynamic Policy for MLB’s Automated Ball-Strike System

Lila Dodson, Zach Sissman

Summary

Evaluates challenge decisions in an automated ball-strike (ABS) system using approximately 2,000 games and 300,000 pitches. A valuable challenge depends on more than the chance of overturning the call.

Methods
  • For each pitch, estimate the probability that the original call was wrong, the run-expectancy gain from an overturn, and the future value of retaining a challenge.
  • Combine the distribution of actual pitch locations with uncertainty about where the batter perceived the pitch.
  • Compare run expectancy if the call is reversed with run expectancy if it stands.
  • Losing the last challenge is more costly. The future cost falls as the game progresses, suggesting a higher confidence threshold is needed earlier in the game.
Findings
  • The official abstract evaluates 497 held-out games: 0.560 run expectancy per game using the model versus 0.459 for observed decisions, a gain of 0.100 (95% interval 0.074–0.128).
  • Model-implied overturn rates were 45.3% versus 54.2%: fewer successful challenges proportionally, but more impactful challenges.
  • The estimate of 1.4 runs per game if every incorrect call were challenged is a hypothetical benchmark.
Limitations

Pitch type was not included in the perception model. The official results measure modeled run expectancy, not additional runs directly observed on the scoreboard.

Potential applications/ideas

NBA/NCAA challenges: adapt this approach to evaluate when to challenge, which game situations make an overturn most valuable, and how confident a coach should be before using a challenge, accounting for each league’s rules.

12. Feasibility of Using Major League Baseball 26-man Roster Spot on an Olympic-caliber Sprinter

Christian Farese

Summary

Models replacing the 13th position player with an Olympic sprinter used only as a pinch runner. It compares the value of the roster spot with gains from stealing second and third.

Methods
  • The 13th position player was defined as the least-used player in each six-game window, excluding the two catchers. Player value was estimated with wins above replacement (WAR).
  • Compare team performance with 12 versus 13 position players.
  • Oblique Seville was the sprinter used in the example. His 90-foot time is estimated as 0.2 seconds faster than the MLB average.
  • Estimates steal success % based on the fastest catcher throw time (J.T. Realmuto)
Findings
  • The selected 13th position player averaged −0.498 WAR per season.
  • The sprinter was estimated to arrive 0.295 seconds ahead of the throw, with approximately 93% success stealing second.
  • Estimated success stealing third was 92.2%, with a smaller but still positive run-value gain.
  • Replacing a player earlier in a game carried a larger cost.
  • Using a single season example with the Miami Marlins, the sprinter strategy improved 98% of modeled seasons.
Limitations
  • Only stealing second and third were modeled.
  • The sprinter was assumed to react like a typical baseball player.
  • Roster cost, defensive adjustments, and batter/pitcher context need fuller treatment.
  • Compares the sprinter to the 13th batter, not a similar pinch runner who can also defend.
Potential applications/ideas

Basketball: explore saving a roster spot for a specialist used solely for tip-offs, out-of-bounds plays, free throws, or other specific situations.

13. Player Latent Ability Through Events (PLATE)

George Perrett · Coauthor: Klint Kanopka

Summary

PLATE jointly estimates pitcher ability and batter characteristics, accounting for the opponents faced. It uses a Bayesian item response tree rather than adding points for plate-appearance outcomes.

Data & methods
  • 2025 MLB and Triple-A data: 367,383 plate appearances, 1,559 pitchers, and 1,124 batters.
  • Conditional branches represent strikeouts, outs, power hits, and runs batted in. Pitcher ability is shared across branches; batter parameters vary by branch.
  • Pitch count enters the model. Hierarchical priors stabilize sparse batter estimates and quantify uncertainty.
Why it matters

A common ability scale supports comparisons across MLB and Triple-A, rather than treating league-specific raw statistics as interchangeable.

Limitations

Limitations include computational cost and communication difficulty. The tree structure is not a universal scoring rubric.

Potential applications/ideas

Basketball:

  • Conference comparisons: treat different conferences as different leagues to estimate player ability on a common scale while accounting for opponents faced.
  • Level translation: explore NCAA to NBA or D2/JUCO to D1 translation using a similar approach to compare player ability across levels of competition.
14. Optimal Serve Placement in Professional Tennis Accounting for Execution Error

Brett Pedersen

Summary

The project studies where a player should aim a serve given their execution error, distinguishing the intended target from where the ball actually lands.

Methods & findings
  • Australian Open data, 2019–21.
  • A generalized additive mixed model estimates point-win probability by bounce location.
  • A three-component Gaussian mixture models serve targeting and precision.
  • Combining both identifies player specific aim points.
  • Second serves favor more conservative targets.
Potential applications/ideas
  • Area of the rim: explore which area of the rim a player should aim for based on their typical shooting error. NOAH’s shot tracking measures arc, depth, and left/right position, which could provide data for this analysis.
  • Pass placement: explore where a passer should aim relative to a moving teammate, balancing the value of leading them toward a scoring opportunity with turnover risk. Account for each passer’s accuracy.
15. Improving the aggregation and evaluation of NBA mock drafts

Jared Fisher

Summary

The project considers two separate tasks: measuring the accuracy of an ordered mock draft and combining multiple mock drafts into a consensus.

Methods
  • Borda count awards points by rank; the example gave 14 points for first place, 13 for second, and so on for the top 14.
  • A comparison measure should account for greater importance near the top, different list lengths, and incomplete rankings.
  • Rank-biased overlap was discussed through agreement on early picks, approximate agreement later in the observed list, and extrapolated agreement beyond it.
  • A Bayesian hierarchical Plackett–Luce model was mentioned as a way to account for team preferences.
Findings
  • Mock drafts became more accurate closer to the draft.
  • Rank-biased distance was favored for evaluating a mock draft as a ranked list.
Potential applications/ideas

Evaluate mock-draft sources using a metric that emphasizes early picks and handles partial lists, then compare consensus methods on historical drafts. Keep source evaluation separate from consensus construction.

  • Analyst evaluation: use NBA GEM’s grading approach as a reference for researching which analysts best predict future player performance, rather than only draft order. Compare their boards with later NBA outcomes and whether they add information beyond consensus.
  • Predicting analyst boards: model how an analyst’s past rankings differ from consensus based on player profiles and preferences, then predict what their next draft board might look like. Test the model on later drafts using only information available before publication.
