UCSD Basketball Implementation Ideas

After attending the Carnegie Mellon Sports Analytics Conference, here are two ideas I think we could feasibly implement. For notes on all the papers, click here.

1. Turn practice and game film into 2D tracking data

Steven Mintz, the NBA’s Senior Manager of Data Strategy and Analytics, presented how the NBA uses Hawk-Eye’s 3D tracking to get more detailed insights into shot contests, defensive pressure, and gravity. Tracking body positions adds information such as the height of a defender’s hand and how they obstruct a shooter’s view.

I talked to Steven after the presentation about what we could implement at UCSD. Given the high cost of the NBA’s Hawk-Eye system, he suggested a computer vision model that produces 2D tracking from video as a more affordable approach.

We could use that approach to turn UCSD practice and game film into player positions and movement on a 2D court. Use BYU Court Lab as an example of the direction we could take.

Suggestion: Build a model for 2D tracking similar to this using UCSD practice film.

Potential applications

  • Movement after a pass and off the ball: examine where players move after passing, how they cut or relocate, and what they do when they do not have the ball. Connect tracking to pass timestamps, tagged manually at first if needed.
  • Team spacing: visualize how spread out the team is and how spacing changes throughout a possession.
  • Matchup zone coverage: estimate how much court area is being guarded and identify potential gaps. Define coverage using defender positions and reachable distances, then check it against film; location alone does not show every area a defender can guard.
  • Player film compilation: try to implement Skye’s project of trimming a full game into an approximately seven-minute clip focused on a specific player. Reliable player identification and a way to select relevant possessions would be needed alongside tracking.

Similar work

SkillCorner’s open basketball data provides tracking outputs and derived events for 10 games from Spain’s professional Liga ACB. It is an example of the kind of data we could aim to produce and use to prototype analyses. The repository provides the outputs, rather than the computer vision model needed to process UCSD film.

Related talk: Basketball tracking.

2. Bayesian player skill estimates

Alexandre Andorra’s talk, Was It the Player or the Team?, explored Bayesian models for estimating soccer players’ scoring skill while accounting for measured team context. Basically, it tries to estimate a player’s underlying scoring skill while accounting for small samples, team context (really important for us!), and prior information. It adjusts for the team factors included in the model, rather than fully isolating a player’s “real skill.”

Evan Miyakawa uses a similar Bayesian approach on EvanMiya. We can use something similar to estimate player efficiency statistics such as three-point percentage (3PT%) and defensive rebound percentage (DRB%), then include these estimates in the dashboard.

For this, it would be helpful to have access to the base version of EvanMiya to compare our predictions with his where comparable metrics are available. He has estimated efficiency statistics such as 3PT%, so we can compare those directly.

Suggestion: Use Bayesian methods and partial pooling to predict statistics for players.
